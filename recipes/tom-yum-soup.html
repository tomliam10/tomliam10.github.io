Tom Yum Soup, Actually Balanced

Hot, sour, fragrant, and clear — not the thick, orange, cream-heavy version most restaurants abroad serve. Here's the real broth technique.

Prep15 min
Cook20 min
Serves4
HeatAdjustable

Photo by Jamie Trinh on Unsplash

Why this recipe works

Tom Yum ("boiled" + "sour/spicy") is built entirely on fresh aromatics simmered briefly, not a paste dumped into stock. The version most people know outside Thailand is thickened with cream or condensed milk (tom yum nam khon) — this is the clear, sharper original (tom yum nam sai), where the broth stays light and every flavor is distinct.

Ingredients

Aromatic base

  • 4 cups chicken or vegetable stock
  • 3 stalks lemongrass, smashed and cut into 2-inch pieces
  • 5 slices galangal (or ginger, less ideal)
  • 4 kaffir lime leaves, torn
  • 3-4 Thai bird's eye chilies, smashed

Soup

  • 8 oz shrimp, peeled (or mushrooms for vegetarian)
  • 1 cup mushrooms, halved (straw or button)
  • 3 tbsp fish sauce
  • 3 tbsp lime juice
  • 1 tbsp Thai chili paste (nam prik pao), optional but recommended
  • Cilantro, to finish

Steps

  1. Build the aromatic base. Bring stock to a boil, then add lemongrass, galangal, kaffir lime leaves, and chilies. Simmer 8-10 minutes to infuse — don't rush this step, it's where the flavor actually comes from.
  2. Add the chili paste. Stir in nam prik pao if using — it adds depth and a subtle smokiness without making the broth heavy.
  3. Cook the mushrooms. Add mushrooms and simmer 3-4 minutes until tender.
  4. Add the shrimp. Add shrimp and cook just until pink and opaque, about 2-3 minutes — overcooked shrimp turns rubbery fast.
  5. Season off the heat. Remove from heat, then stir in fish sauce and lime juice. Adding lime juice off-heat keeps its brightness from cooking off.
  6. Taste and adjust. It should be aggressively sour and salty with a building heat — add more lime for sour, more fish sauce for salt, more chili for heat.
  7. Serve immediately, topped with cilantro. The lemongrass, galangal, and lime leaves are aromatics, not meant to be eaten — leave them in for presentation or fish them out first.

Notes

Galangal looks like ginger but tastes sharper and more piney — they're not interchangeable in a pinch the way recipes sometimes claim, though ginger works if it's truly all you have. Frozen galangal, sold at most Asian grocery stores, works fine and keeps for months. This soup lives or dies on the fish sauce — we use Three Crabs or Megachef at home, and the rounder, less harsh flavor is noticeable in a broth this simple.
